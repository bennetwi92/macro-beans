SHELL vs BP — WHAT'S DRIVING THE SWINGS

A look at the last year of Shell against BP — the two London oil giants. Both ride the crude-oil price together, so the interesting question is what makes them pull apart. Here's the story behind each swing.

THE SHORT VERSION

Over the last year BP rose about +42% while Shell rose about +23% — BP nearly doubled Shell's return. So this pair was not a quiet, range-bound relationship: it trended hard in BP's favour, with sharp reversals around company results and a mid-year oil shock.

Three things drove the gap: a long-running takeover story around BP, a capital-returns split (Shell kept buying back shares while BP paused its buyback), and a spike in the oil price that helped the more oil-sensitive of the two — BP — the most.

WHAT MAKES THE TWO MOVE APART

Shell and BP are both integrated oil majors, so most days they move together with the oil price — they're about 80% correlated. The Shell / BP pair portfolio is beta-hedged, which strips out that shared oil move and leaves only the part that's specific to each company. That leftover part is what this report is about.

BP is the more sensitive, higher-risk name. It carries more debt, is more geared to the oil price, looks cheaper, and spent the year wrapped in takeover talk. That means it tends to move more than Shell — leading the way up when oil and sentiment are rising, and falling faster when they turn.

Shell is the steadier, higher-quality name. Stronger cash generation, lower debt, and a consistent share buyback. It tends to win when the two diverge on company fundamentals, and to hold up better on the way down.

1 · THE TAKEOVER STORY

The single biggest reason BP outran Shell was a year-long question hanging over BP: would somebody buy it? Activist investor Elliott built a stake of around 5% early in 2025 and pushed BP to cut costs, sell assets and return more cash — classic pressure that lifts a cheap, unloved share.

In late June 2025 the speculation went up a gear when reports claimed Shell itself was looking at BP. Shell quickly issued a formal denial under the UK Takeover Code — which legally barred it from bidding for six months, until 26 December. That denial briefly cooled things, but it also left BP wearing a "could be bought" premium for the rest of the year. The talk flared up again as that six-month ban expired, and again when BP announced a new chief executive (Meg O'Neill, starting April 2026) — every twist gave BP another nudge ahead of Shell.

The flip side showed up in early December 2025, when Shell's head of dealmaking left after the CEO blocked an internal push to buy BP. That signalled no Shell bid was coming, the takeover premium deflated, and Shell briefly outperformed.

2 · THE CASH-RETURNS SPLIT

The cleanest fundamental swing came in February 2026. On the 10th, BP reported weak full-year results and suspended its share buyback to focus on paying down debt — its shares fell several percent, the worst single day for BP relative to Shell all year. Shell, by contrast, was generating strong cash and launched a fresh buyback. One company was handing money back to shareholders while the other pulled it away, and Shell sharply outperformed for a few weeks.

3 · THE OIL SHOCK

At the end of February 2026 conflict involving Iran disrupted oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz — a critical shipping route — and the oil price rocketed, with Brent crude trading up towards $120–$128 a barrel in its biggest monthly jump on record. Because BP is the more oil-sensitive of the two, it shot ahead through March (up about +22% against Shell's +13%).

That advantage didn't last. As ceasefire talks emerged and the oil price came back down through May and into June, BP gave back its gains faster than Shell — the same sensitivity that helped it on the way up hurt it on the way down. By June the gap had partly closed again.

SO WHAT ACTUALLY MOVES THIS PAIR?

Most of BP's big year was a re-rating: a cheap, heavily-indebted, takeover-target share catching up — helped along by a once-in-a-decade oil spike that suits the more oil-sensitive name.

But the cleanest swings between the two were company events, not the oil price: the takeover denials and flare-ups, and above all BP pausing its buyback while Shell kept buying. If you're watching this pair, the days that matter most are results days and any takeover headline — the oil moves tend to wash out once you hedge one against the other.
